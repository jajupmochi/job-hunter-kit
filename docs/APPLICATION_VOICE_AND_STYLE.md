# Writing that sounds like a person

> Every example in this kit is invented. See the disclaimer in the [README](../README.md).

The single most-corrected thing in any AI-assisted application. **A letter that reads as
machine-written is worse than a plain one**, because the reader stops trusting the content.

## Part 1 · The patterns that read as generated

Each of these is a shape, not a word. Learn the shape and you will catch versions this list
does not contain.

**A comma followed by a short trailing clause.** "I built the pipeline, which was a great
learning experience." The clause adds nothing and the rhythm is unmistakable. Cut it, or make
it its own sentence with something in it.

**A colon or semicolon opening a clause after a complete sentence.** "The role is a strong
fit: it combines research with delivery." Use a full stop.

**Mid-sentence appositives between commas.** "Your team, a leader in this space, is exactly
where I want to be." Two commas around a compliment is the most common generated construction
there is.

**Verbless lists hung off a preposition.** "Experience with data pipelines, model training,
stakeholder communication, and documentation." Give each item a verb, or cut to the two that
matter.

**Triads.** "Rigorous, collaborative, and impact-driven." Three adjectives in a row is a rhythm
almost nobody uses when speaking.

**Sentences that are too smooth and too long.** If a sentence glides and you cannot say what
new fact it added, it is decoration. **Aim for a mean of about sixteen words.** Short sentences
read as confident; long ones read as generated.

**Summary paragraphs.** "In conclusion", "To summarise", "Overall". If the letter needs a
summary it is too long, and the summary is what to cut.

**Grand or absolute claims.** "Perfectly aligned", "uniquely positioned", "deeply passionate".
An overclaim invites the reader to test it.

## Part 2 · Keep your own voice

**If your natural register is formal, stay formal.** The goal is not casual, it is *yours*. A
formal sentence that is specific beats a breezy one that is empty.

**If you are writing in a second language, that is not a defect to correct.** The rules above
are about emptiness and rhythm, not about native fluency, and they apply to first-language
writers just as often.

**Reuse your own phrasing verbatim.** If you have already written a sentence that says the
thing, keep it exactly. An "improved" version is usually a more generic version. See
[G-08](../lessons/G-08-change-only-what-the-rule-requires.md).

**The test is reading it aloud.** Where you stumble, or where you would not say it to a person,
rewrite. This catches more than any list.

## Part 3 · Keep a truth-anchor list

The most valuable page in your own private workspace is a short list of **facts about your work
that keep getting overstated**, with the correct wording next to each.

Write it as you catch the drift, one line each: the claim as it wrongly appeared, and the
sentence you actually stand behind.

Two invented examples of the shape:

> **Not** "I built the reporting platform." **Say** "I was one of three people who built it;
> the ingestion layer was mine."

> **Not** "deployed to production." **Say** "running in internal testing since March."

**This list belongs in your private workspace, never in a public repository**, because it is
a precise inventory of your real work. See [G-07](../lessons/G-07-every-claim-has-a-source.md)
and [PRIVACY_CHECKLIST.md](PRIVACY_CHECKLIST.md).

## Part 4 · Conventions worth fixing once

- **No em-dashes and no double hyphens** in anything a person will read. They are a strong
  generated-text signal and a comma or a full stop always works.
- **No parenthetical asides.** If it matters, it is a sentence. If not, cut it.
- **Do not end on a preposition** where a small rearrangement avoids it.
- **Do not volunteer an unasked weakness.** Naming one deliberate gap is
  [G-05](../lessons/G-05-name-the-gap.md); apologising is not.
- **Use "we" for genuine team work**, and say which part was yours.
