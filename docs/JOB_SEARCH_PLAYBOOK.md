# Reaching people, not just postings

> Every example in this kit is invented. See the disclaimer in the [README](../README.md).

A job board tells you a role exists. A person tells you what it is actually for, whether the
team is any good, and sometimes that it exists before it is posted.

**Contacting a named person converts far better than submitting through a portal.** Track your
own two numbers, per application, and let them decide how you spend your time. Do not take
anybody else's ratio, including the one whose search produced this kit.

## Where the people are

**Hiring announcements.** People on hiring teams announce openings in public, often before the
formal advert and often with more honesty about what the job is. These are answered by the
person doing the hiring, and they are far less crowded than the board listing.

**Alumni.** Everywhere you studied has a searchable alumni network. Filter by where you want
to work and what you want to do. **An alumnus is a stranger with a reason to reply.**

**Former colleagues, and their colleagues.** People who have worked with you can speak to your
work, which no application can. This includes people from short collaborations.

**Where people go next.** Look at where people from your previous groups ended up. Their
trajectories are a map of the paths actually available to someone with your background, which
is a different and more useful question than which jobs are open today.

**People who mention mentoring.** Someone who describes themselves as mentoring has told you
they are willing to spend time on people like you. That is a stronger predictor of a reply than
any measure of fit.

## Writing to someone

**Short.** Four or five sentences. Every extra paragraph lowers the chance of a reply.

**Say who you are in one clause**, say what you are interested in and why it is them
specifically, ask one small question, and stop.

**Ask for a conversation, not a job.** People cannot give you a job. They can give you twenty
minutes, and twenty minutes is where the useful information is.

**Never attach an application to a first message.** It converts a conversation into an
obligation.

## When there is no reply

Most messages get no reply. **This is not a verdict on you.** People are busy, timing is mostly
luck, and the only reasonable response is to keep going, politely.

One follow-up after a week or two is fine. A second is not.

## Vocabulary

If you are moving between sectors, the same work has different names in each. Many readers use
their sector's vocabulary daily, so it reads as familiar rather than as posturing, and some
readers will not care either way.

**The test is whether the sentence is still true after the rewrite.** If it is, use the words
your reader uses. If it is not, do not use them. Some people find this uncomfortable, and that
discomfort is worth taking seriously rather than arguing away: it is usually a signal that a
specific rewrite went too far, not that the practice is wrong.

## The hard rule

**You send everything.** No agent, no tool, no script sends a message, a connection request, a
comment or an application on your behalf. Drafts are produced; you read them, change what does
not sound like you, and send them yourself.

This is not caution about tooling. It is that **the thing being automated is a relationship**,
and a message you did not write is a relationship you did not start.

## Etiquette

- If a poster invites replies, a short specific comment is fine. If they ask for direct
  messages or a link, use that instead. **Do not post a placeholder comment** to be noticed.
- Do not contact several people at one employer about the same role at once.
- If someone helps you, tell them what happened. Almost nobody does this, and it is the single
  cheapest thing that turns one favour into a relationship.
